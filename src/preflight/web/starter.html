The audience you already have is the one you keep ignoring

Hi {{ subscriber.first_name }} — I spent most of last quarter trying to solve the wrong problem. Every week I opened the dashboard, looked at the subscriber count, and treated the number on that card as the only thing worth moving. New names felt like progress. Everything else felt like maintenance.

It took a genuinely embarrassing amount of time to notice that the thousand people who had already said yes were reading less of what I sent than they had a year earlier, and that no amount of new signups was going to fix a room that was quietly emptying out the back.

The number that actually moved

I went back through six months of issues and pulled one figure for each: how many people who opened it clicked anything at all. Not opens, which lie now. Not the list size, which flatters. Just the fraction of people who arrived and then did something.

That number had halved. It had halved while the list grew by four hundred people, which is exactly why I had not seen it. The growth chart was covering for the engagement chart, and I had built a habit of looking at the one that made me feel better.

A list that grows while engagement falls is not growing. It is being refilled, at the same rate it leaks, by people who have not yet learned to ignore you.

What I changed, in order

The first change was the least comfortable one: I stopped sending on a schedule. For two years I had shipped every Tuesday whether or not I had something, and roughly a third of those issues existed because it was Tuesday. Those are the issues that teach people to stop opening.

The second was to write the first line last. I had been opening with context — where I had been, what had been busy, why this one was late. Nobody subscribed for that. Now the first sentence is the thing I would say if I had one sentence, and everything explaining it comes after.

The third took a weekend: I read the last thirty issues on a phone, in the dark, on the sofa, which is where most of you actually read them. Four of them were close to unreadable. I had been writing for a laptop at a desk in the afternoon, which is a room almost none of my readers are sitting in.

The part I got wrong

For about a month I over-corrected and sent almost nothing, on the theory that scarcity would do the work. It did not. Engagement recovers when the writing is worth opening, not when it is rare, and a newsletter nobody has heard from in five weeks is not scarce — it is forgotten.

What settled it was a cadence I could actually sustain with something to say: roughly every ten days, occasionally sooner when something genuinely happened. Six months in, click rate is above where it was two years ago, on a list that is smaller than it was in March. I am fine with that trade and I would make it again.
