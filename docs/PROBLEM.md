# The problem

## Send-button anxiety

A broadcast is the least reversible thing a creator does. A blog post gets
edited, a tweet gets deleted; an email that went to 12,000 people is simply out
there. So the last twenty minutes before a send are spent not writing, but
checking — and checking badly, because the tools do not exist.

The manual routine, as practised:

| Step | Typical time | What it actually catches |
| --- | ---: | --- |
| Send a test to yourself | 2–3 min | Layout in *one* client, in *one* colour scheme |
| Forward to a second address | 2–4 min | Maybe Outlook, maybe not |
| Click every link | 4–10 min | Scales linearly with links; skipped when rushed |
| Re-read for typos | 5–15 min | Nothing structural |
| Squint at it on a phone | 1–2 min | Not dark mode, which is the common failure |
| **Total** | **20–45 min** | **A fraction of what breaks** |

The tax is real and recurring, and it is paid in the worst possible currency:
the hour before shipping, when attention is lowest and pressure is highest.

The routine also misses the things that actually go wrong. Nobody eyeballs a
contrast ratio. Nobody previews forced dark mode, which is where the most common
silent failure lives — text pinned to a colour, sitting on a background the
client repaints, so it renders black-on-black for roughly half the audience.
Nobody notices an unclosed `{{ subscriber.first_name` until the replies arrive.

**What we actually know.** Published research on email marketers puts manual
pre-send checking at roughly **20–25%** of them. That is an external figure about
marketers in general, not measured on one platform's creators, and it says how many rather
than how long — the time range above remains an estimate from the routine's
steps.

**And what walking the product shows.** Going through a shipping platform's publish flow as a
creator: subject line, canvas, Continue, Send email, Continue. Nothing anywhere
says the broadcast is in good shape. You do not send because you are confident;
you send because there is nothing left to click.

That reframes the problem. Creators are not hunting for defects when they mail
themselves a test — they are manufacturing a reassurance by hand because the
product does not supply it. The absence of a signal is the problem, and the
defects are only what makes the absence expensive.

## How common is it, really?

`evals/PREVALENCE_REPORT.md` is generated by `scripts/run_prevalence_study.py`
and is the honest answer, including when the honest answer is "not enough data
yet".

What the first pass established is mostly **methodological**, and it matters:

> Newsletter *archive pages* are not the emails that were sent. Their CSS is
> generated by the publisher's web app. In the six real pages sampled, **0%**
> showed dark-mode collapse — because web pages paint their own backgrounds and
> email templates frequently do not.

That single result is why the study separates `sent_email` from `web_archive`
sources and refuses to blend them. A prevalence number built from archive pages
would be confidently, measurably wrong about the exact failure mode the tool
exists to catch.

The headline figure stays empty until real exports land in `evals/real/`. This
is deliberate: a fabricated "61% fail WCAG AA" is the same category of error as
the spam score this tool refuses to invent (D8).

## Who this is for

Two segments, with genuinely opposing needs. Serving an average of them is what
produced the unresolved `--aggressive` flag before it had a rationale.

### Hobbyist — under ~2,000 subscribers

Writes in the Wren composer, uses a stock template, has no CSS opinions and no
one to ask. Ships weekly-ish, alone, usually late at night.

- **Wants the problem gone, not explained.** One button, applied, move on.
- **High tolerance for us touching the markup** — they did not write it and have
  no attachment to it.
- **Low tolerance for vocabulary.** "WCAG AA 3.1:1" means nothing; "this text
  will be hard to read" does.
- Failure mode we must avoid: a wall of findings that makes them feel the email
  is broken when it is fine.

### Professional — 10,000+ subscribers

Hand-built or agency template, brand guidelines, sometimes a designer. The
broadcast is revenue. Sends on a schedule, often reviewed by someone else.

- **Wants a diff, not a fix.** Show what changed and let them approve it.
- **Zero tolerance for unrequested CSS mutation.** A stylesheet rewrite that
  silently alters brand colours is worse than the bug it fixed.
- **Wants the reasoning**, because they may have to defend the change.
- Failure mode we must avoid: touching the `<style>` block without being asked.

### What this settles

`--aggressive` now has a rationale instead of being a hedge. The default fix
never touches the creator's stylesheet; the opt-in flag does. That is the
Professional's guarantee, and it is enforced in code: `Finding.requires_aggressive`
keeps stylesheet-only repairs out of the one-click button, so the button cannot
advertise a fix it will not apply (D29).

The two segments also explain the verdict model. HOLD/REVIEW/READY is a decision
for the Hobbyist; the itemised findings underneath are the Professional's diff.

## Success metrics

None of these is F1. F1 measures the engine; these measure whether the product
works. Every rate is reported with the `n` **it was actually computed from**,
from opt-in, content-free telemetry (D25, D31).

Four of the five are computable today. **Audit rate is not**, and saying
otherwise would be the same error this document keeps warning about: its
denominator is drafts that were *never audited*, which is invisible from inside
a tool you had to choose to run. It becomes measurable the day this ships inside
the composer, and not before — which is itself an argument for the distribution
section below.

| Metric | Definition | Why it is the one that matters |
| --- | --- | --- |
| **Audit rate** *(needs the composer)* | Drafts running preflight ÷ drafts sent | If nobody runs it, accuracy is irrelevant. The first metric to move — and the one we cannot see yet. |
| **Action rate on HOLD** | HOLDs where the creator changed something before sending | The product working as intended. |
| **Fix acceptance rate** | One-click repairs kept ÷ (kept + undone) | Whether our repairs are actually wanted. A low rate means we are fixing the wrong things, or badly. |
| **Override rate** | HOLDs sent anyway ÷ all HOLDs | **The credibility metric.** A creator who overrides is telling us the verdict was wrong, and they are usually right. |
| **Time-to-send delta** | Median seconds from first audit to resolution | The cost side. A four-minute tax kills adoption at any accuracy. |

Override rate is the one to watch. Everything else can look healthy while the
tool is quietly training people to ignore it.

### Kill criteria

Stated in advance, so they cannot be renegotiated later:

- **Override rate > 40%** — creators do not believe the verdicts. Stop shipping
  checks; recalibrate against what they overrode. A tool that cries wolf gets
  uninstalled, and the override log is the earliest evidence of it.
- **Any single check dismissed as wrong more than 40% of the time** — that check
  is recalibrated or retired, and the rest of the product carries on. This is
  the more useful half of the criterion above: overriding a whole send says we
  are wrong somewhere, while waving through one finding says exactly where.
  Nothing is judged below twenty decisions, because three dismissals is a rumour
  and retiring a working check on a rumour is the more expensive mistake. The
  automatic version exists and is off by default: a check that disables itself
  is a check nobody notices is gone.
- **Fix acceptance < 50%** — the auto-fixer is not wanted. Fall back to
  flagging only, and stop advertising one-click repair.
- **Audit rate < 10% after distribution** — the problem is not felt where we put
  the tool, or the tool is in the wrong place. Question the placement before the
  checks. *(Only checkable once embedded; until then this criterion is armed but
  not measurable, and it is listed rather than quietly dropped.)*
- **Median time-to-resolve > 120s** — we replaced a 20-minute tax with a
  2-minute one and called it a win. Below the fold of what creators will accept.

## Distribution

Impact = **reach × accuracy**. This repo has spent its effort entirely on the
second term, and reach is currently near zero.

| Surface | Reach | Fidelity | Status |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| CLI | Very low — creators do not use terminals | Full | Built |
| Local web UI | Low — still requires running a server | Full | Built |
| **Wren pre-send modal** | **Highest** — every broadcast passes through it | Full | Not built |
| CI / GitHub Action | Low, but high-value for template teams | Full | Not built |
| Public web checker | Medium, good for acquisition | Reduced (no Liquid context) | Not built |

The pre-send modal is the only surface where this becomes a product rather than
a tool: it sits exactly at the moment of value, needs no installation, and is the
one place the audit rate can approach 100%.

Nothing about the engine changes for it. `web/app.py` already proves the point —
it parses a request, calls `audit_html` / `fix_document`, and serialises. The
2-second pre-send SLA (D18) exists precisely so this can run inline without
anyone noticing.

## Open questions

Honest list of what is not yet known, in the order it should be answered:

1. **Is 20–45 minutes real?** Estimated, not observed. Ten creators timed on
   their actual pre-send routine would replace the weakest number here.
2. **Is pre-send QA the top pain?** Never validated against growth,
   deliverability reputation, segmentation, or monetisation. It is plausible,
   not established.
3. **What is the real prevalence in sent email?** Blocked on a corpus of real
   exports. The harness is built and waiting.
4. **Does the dark-mode check match real clients?** Verified against a model of
   forced dark mode, not against Gmail, Outlook and Apple Mail rendering the
   same file.
5. **Which findings do creators actually act on?** The telemetry can answer this
   the moment there is traffic. Any check with a persistently low action rate
   should be deleted, not tuned.
